        01 Definition & subtypes 1.5 min
        Discuss first

        "What actually makes a fever 'unknown origin'? What's the minimum workup threshold before it qualifies as FUO — and why does the 3-week mark matter clinically?"

        Classic FUO: Temperature >38.3°C (>101°F) documented on ≥2 occasions, duration >3 weeks, no diagnosis after adequate evaluation — defined as ≥3 outpatient visits, ≥3 inpatient days, or ≥1 week of structured ambulatory investigation.

        The 3-week threshold excludes self-limited viral illness. The "adequate evaluation" clause is the real key — it's not FUO if nobody looked carefully. (Petersdorf & Beeson, 1961; updated Durack & Street, 1991)

        Nosocomial

        Fever >3 days in hospital, afebrile on admission. Top considerations: drug fever, C. diff, DVT/PE, line infection, occult abscess. Different workup entirely.

        Neutropenic — emergency

        ANC <500. Antipseudomonal antibiotics within 1 hour. This is a protocol, not a judgment call. Mortality increases with every hour of delay.

        HIV-associated

        Fever >4 weeks (or >3 days if hospitalized). Expand the differential: MAC, CMV, disseminated histo/cocci, PJP, lymphoma, IRIS.

        03 History & exam — where diagnoses hide 3 min
        Discuss first

        "Your intern wants to order 'the FUO panel' — ESR, ANA, ANCA, SPEP, ferritin, all serologies at once. What do you say, and why does the order of operations matter?"

        "The history is the answer. The labs are the confirmation."

        A single exposure detail — cat scratch, raw milk, tick bite, new lisinopril, demolition site — can collapse a 68-disease differential in seconds. No lab panel can do that.

        History domains to interrogate every time
        Travel (past 12 months) Animal contact Tick / mosquito exposure Raw foods / milk / shellfish New medications (3–6 wk) Sexual history / STI risk TB contacts / incarceration Occupation / demolition / caves River valley / freshwater exposure Alcohol / liver disease Implants / prosthetics / recent dental Family history periodic fevers
        Physical exam — what gets missed
        • Skin in full light: Janeway lesions (painless palmar macules = endocarditis), Osler nodes (painful fingertip nodules), splinter hemorrhages, salmon/evanescent rash (Still's), petechiae (RMSF, meningococcemia), erythema nodosum
        • All lymph node chains: posterior cervical, supraclavicular, epitrochlear, popliteal — not just groin and axilla
        • Temporal arteries in any patient ≥50 with headache: tenderness, induration, absent pulse, scalp tenderness
        • Inside the mouth: dental abscess, gingival findings — often the culprit when present
        • Fundoscope: Roth spots (endocarditis), choroidal lesions (disseminated fungal / miliary TB)
        Fever pattern — don't suppress it

        Scheduled antipyretics erase a diagnostic signal. Quotidian (spikes returning to baseline daily) = Adult Still's. Pel-Ebstein (days of fever / days of afebrile, cycling) = Hodgkin. Tertian (q48h) = P. vivax / falciparum. Undulant = brucellosis. Drenching sweats at peak = lymphoma or TB.

        04 Staged workup — following the PDC 3 min

        PDC = Potentially Diagnostic Clue — any history finding, physical finding, or lab result that narrows the differential. The entire strategy is: find a PDC, follow it with targeted testing, reassess. Do not order everything at once.

        Stage 1 — Initial evaluation
        • CBC with diff, CMP, LFTs, LDH, ferritin, ESR, CRP, UA + culture
        • Blood cultures ×3 from separate sites — BEFORE any antibiotics
        • CXR; CT chest if abnormal or high suspicion
        • CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast
        • HIV 4th-gen Ag/Ab, IGRA (QuantiFERON), EBV/CMV serology
        Stage 2 — If no PDC after Stage 1
        • Targeted serology based on exposure history (Brucella, Bartonella/Coxiella, Lyme two-tier, etc.)
        • ANA, ANCA, RF, anti-dsDNA, complement if inflammatory suspected
        • SPEP + UPEP if malignancy / plasma cell disorder suspected
        • TTE → TEE if endocarditis remains on list despite negative blood cultures
        • Stop non-essential medications × 72 h to test for drug fever
        Stage 3 — Persistent FUO, advanced workup
        • FDG-PET/CT — highest single-test yield after Stage 1 fails; identifies metabolically active tissue (inflammation, malignancy, occult abscess) and guides biopsy site
        • Lymph node biopsy: excisional preferred. High yield: posterior cervical, supraclavicular, epitrochlear. Avoid: inguinal, axillary (low specificity, reactive changes)
        • Temporal artery biopsy in any patient ≥50 with elevated ESR
        • Bone marrow biopsy (pancytopenia, suspected miliary TB, culture-negative endocarditis)
        • Liver biopsy if granulomatous hepatitis suspected (elevated alk phos, hepatomegaly)
        PET-CT pearl

        FDG-PET is the most useful advanced imaging in FUO — not just CT or MRI alone. Best evidence: use it when CRP/ESR are elevated and no localizing clue found after Stage 1–2 workup. It detects occult lymphoma, large-vessel vasculitis (e.g., aortitis in GCA), active granulomas, and abscess that may be invisible on standard cross-sectional imaging.

        05 What NOT to do — and why 2 min
        Discuss first

        "Your patient spikes to 39.9°C at 2 AM. The nurse calls asking for Tylenol orders. What do you say — and if the answer is 'no,' how do you explain it to the patient?"

        ✕ Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics for undiagnosed FUO

        Sterilizes blood cultures. Masks fever pattern. Creates false reassurance of "responding to treatment." The only exceptions are genuine emergencies where delay is life-threatening — neutropenic fever, suspected RMSF, GCA with visual loss (see Section 6).

        ✕ Empiric glucocorticoids before a diagnosis

        Steroids lower fever in infection, malignancy, and inflammatory disease alike — they are not a diagnostic test. Worse: steroids can lyse lymphoma cells and dramatically reduce biopsy yield. Exception: GCA with active visual symptoms — treat now, biopsy within 1–2 weeks — histopathology remains interpretable.

        ✕ Scheduled antipyretics masking fever pattern

        Quotidian = Still's. Pel-Ebstein = Hodgkin. Tertian = malaria. Undulant = brucellosis. Scheduled Tylenol erases these signals. Use only PRN for comfort at very high temperatures.

        ✕ Anchoring on a nonspecific positive test

        ANA 1:80, positive monospot, mildly elevated ferritin — these are clues, not diagnoses. Premature closure on a low-specificity result causes anchoring and delays the real diagnosis. Continue the systematic evaluation.

        ✕ Skip the mouth and the skin

        Dental abscess, Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, splinter hemorrhages, salmon rash, petechiae — the diagnosis is sometimes visible if you look. 60 seconds, every time.

        06 When to treat empirically — the exceptions 2 min
        Discuss first

        "67-year-old from Oklahoma, 9 days of fever, headache, thrombocytopenia, and transaminitis. No rash yet. Walk me through your decision about empiric treatment."

        🔴 Neutropenic fever (ANC <500)

        This is a protocol, not a clinical judgment. Antipseudomonal beta-lactam (cefepime, pip-tazo, meropenem based on MASCC risk) within 1 hour. Start before results are back.

        🔴 GCA with threatened vision

        Amaurosis fugax, diplopia, or monocular visual loss in a patient ≥50 with elevated ESR → prednisone 60 mg PO (or methylprednisolone 1 g IV × 3) today. Do not wait for biopsy. Temporal artery biopsy remains interpretable for 1–2 weeks after steroids are started.

        ⚠ RMSF / tick-borne — South-Central US, tick exposure

        Tick exposure + fever + thrombocytopenia + transaminitis (± headache) in OK/AR/MO/KS/TN → doxycycline 100 mg PO/IV BID now. Serology is negative in the first 7–10 days. Mortality climbs sharply after day 5 — you cannot wait for confirmatory results.

        ⚠ Miliary / disseminated TB — deteriorating immunosuppressed patient

        Immunosuppressed, weight loss + night sweats + bilateral infiltrates, clinical deterioration → empiric RIPE before AFB culture (6–8 weeks). Send sputum AFB ×3, urine AFB, blood AFB, bone marrow culture first — then treat.

        ⚠ HLH — ferritin >10,000 + cytopenias + splenomegaly

        This is HLH until proven otherwise. Immediate hematology + rheumatology co-management. Add triglycerides, fibrinogen, NK activity, sIL-2R. Do not treat as refractory sepsis without excluding HLH — it will not respond and the delay is fatal.

        07 Take-home pearls 1 min
        • FUO is almost always an atypical presentation of a common disease — TB without pulmonary symptoms, endocarditis without a murmur, lymphoma without palpable LAD. Think common diseases presenting uncommonly.
        • 40% of FUO cases never get a diagnosis and many of those resolve spontaneously. An undiagnosed FUO is not automatically a death sentence.
        • History first, labs second. A single exposure detail collapses the differential instantly. No shotgun panel can do that — and shotgun panels generate false positives that anchor thinking.
        • Ferritin >10,000 is approximately 90% sensitive for HLH in adults with systemic inflammation. Always check it in FUO; it also points toward Still's disease and lymphoma.
        • Serial physical exams reveal findings that a single admission exam misses — new lymph node, evolving murmur, temporal artery tenderness. Revisit the exam each day.
        • The naproxen test: 3 days of naproxen reliably suppress fever → malignant etiology more likely than infectious. Low evidence, but a useful gestalt adjunct.
        • Lymph node biopsy site matters: posterior cervical, supraclavicular, epitrochlear = high diagnostic yield. Inguinal, axillary = avoid if possible (reactive changes, low specificity).
        • When FUO persists for months without a diagnosis, revisit the medication list — drug fever can come from an agent started years ago (e.g., hydralazine, phenytoin, allopurinol). Also consider factitious fever in healthcare workers.
